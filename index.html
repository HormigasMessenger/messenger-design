Systems / message delivery

Below the waterline

How a chat backend delivers every message at least once over a Postgres outbox and a Redis watermark — and stays shaped for the Kafka it hasn't moved to yet.

Hormigas Messenger · substrate v1.4.6 · validated @ 2000 msg/s

A one-to-one chat looks trivial until you count what has to survive a dropped socket. A message accepted from a sender must reach its recipient eventually, even if that recipient is asleep, offline, on a train, or reconnecting three times a minute. The honest way to say this is: the system owes an at-least-once guarantee, and it has to honor it without letting the machinery that guarantees it grow without bound.

Our delivery layer is two stores, one watermark, and a small fleet of batchers. This is the design in detail — the parts, the one trade-off that shapes all the others, and why the whole thing is deliberately built to be thrown away in favor of a partitioned log.

01 — Two stores

Durable truth and a delivery buffer

Every accepted message is written to two places in a single transaction. The first is message_history — the durable record of what was said, keyed for retrieval by conversation. The second is the outbox — one row per recipient, a transient instruction that says "this person still needs to receive this."

The two are not redundant. History is the truth; the outbox is a to-do list. History is read back by clients on demand and aged out slowly by a retention clock. The outbox is drained aggressively and, ideally, empty. Critically, the history insert runs first and in the same transaction as the outbox insert — so a message can never be queued for delivery without already being durably recorded.

That single ordering decision is the backbone of the whole guarantee. Delivery can fail, retry, storm, or be abandoned entirely — none of it can lose a message, because losing a delivery row never touches the history row that was written before it. We will lean on this fact repeatedly.

The outbox id is a BIGSERIAL — a monotonic integer. That is not an incidental choice. It is a per-buffer offset, and treating it as one is what keeps this design a rehearsal for Kafka rather than a detour away from it.

02 — Delivery

Two paths to the socket

A message reaches an online recipient by one of two routes. When the recipient is connected at the moment of send, a fast path hands the frame straight to their WebSocket, decoupled from the persistence write so a slow disk never stalls a live conversation. Everything else — offline recipients, missed sends, retries — is the job of the outbox poller.

The poller wakes on a short interval, claims a batch of the oldest un-delivered rows under a visibility lease, and delivers them concurrently. Oldest-first is a rule, not an optimization: reading the buffer from the bottom up is what makes the watermark below meaningful.

A delivered row is not deleted on acknowledgement. It is deleted by garbage collection, and only once everyone below it in the buffer has been dealt with. To understand why, you have to look at the waterline.

03 — The watermark

Tetris, and the safe-delete line

The outbox is one table shared by every recipient, ordered by that monotonic id. Garbage collection is a single, cheap, sequential statement:

# reclaim everything below the global safe-delete line
DELETE FROM outbox WHERE id < safe_delete_id;

The whole trick is computing safe_delete_id. In Redis we keep, per connected client, the lowest outbox id it has not yet acknowledged — its pin. The global safe-delete line is the minimum pin across all clients. Nothing at or above the lowest outstanding message may be deleted, because some client still needs it; everything below it is, by definition, delivered-and-acknowledged for everyone, and can go.

We call the bookkeeping Tetris: rows stack up by id, and the floor rises underneath them as the laggards catch up. A prefix delete against a shared, monotonic id space is about the cheapest reclaim a database can do — no per-row status scan, no random deletes, portable to any store that can express "less than."

One shared minimum is the design's great simplicity — and its one structural weakness.

Because safe_delete_id is a single global minimum, a single stuck recipient holds the line down for everyone. If one online client stops acknowledging — a dead socket that still looks alive, a device wedged mid-reconnect — its pin freezes, the waterline can't rise, and the outbox grows underneath a floor that won't move. Delivery to every other recipient keeps working; their rows just can't be reclaimed. This is classic head-of-line blocking, and at a shared-id-space scale it is unavoidable by construction.

04 — The trade-off

Reconnect churn vs. outbox volume

We could dissolve head-of-line blocking today with clever Postgres — per-row delete-on-ack, an online-gated fetch skip, sharded pin sets. We deliberately don't. Every one of those is throwaway work that a partitioned log makes irrelevant, and each adds a moving part to the exact subsystem we intend to replace. The rule we hold: keep the reclaim portable — one table, one id space, one sequential delete — and pay elsewhere.

Where we pay is a policy at the delivery edge. When a message has been re-delivered more than a small threshold of times without being acknowledged, its recipient is not "briefly behind" — it is the frozen pin holding down the waterline. So we abandon it: advance the watermark past it deterministically, and break its socket. The dropped delivery is not lost — it is still in history — and the client recovers it on reconnect (§05). Breaking one stuck socket lets the floor rise for everyone.

the balance

A tighter drop keeps the outbox small but forces more reconnects. A looser drop tolerates a bigger buffer to spare the churn. The lever that sets this balance is the per-message delivery-attempts cap, backed by three independent guards that reap unhealthy connections on different signals.

Three guards, one goal

  • Idle reaper — closes sockets that stopped answering pings. Signal: silence. It clears zombies.
  • Overload reaper — drops a client whose un-acknowledged backlog exceeds max-pending. Signal: depth. A count-based guardrail against one client's set growing without bound.
  • Attempts drop — the primary lever. Abandons the recipient of a message re-delivered past max-delivery-attempts. Signal: staleness of the oldest outstanding row. This is the one that keeps the waterline moving.

They run in parallel on different clocks and never conflict; a client can trip two at once and the second drop is simply a no-op. There is no single "client health" controller — three heuristics, each cheap, is enough until the log arrives.

The grace question

The obvious refinement is a reconnect-grace window: don't abandon a client that only just reconnected — give it a moment to acknowledge the tail before judging it. We built the knob and measured it. Under sustained load it is counterproductive: a client that can't keep up now won't keep up in another second either, and the grace window merely delays the inevitable drop while the poller re-delivers its un-acked rows in the meantime — a re-delivery storm that inflates both the buffer and the churn it was meant to reduce.

So the grace defaults to zero: abandon immediately, recover via history. The knob survives for the case it actually helps — a transient blip at low load, where a healthy client hiccups for under a second and a small grace saves it a needless reconnect. Under saturation, aggression wins, because the recovery path is cheap.

05 — The guarantee

Why dropping a delivery loses nothing

Everything above hinges on one claim: abandoning a delivery is safe. It is safe because of the ordering in §01 and two invariants that never bend.

  • Durability is unconditional. The message is in message_history, written in the same transaction as — and before — its outbox row. No delivery outcome can un-write it.
  • Garbage collection only ever touches the outbox. The waterline delete, and the age-based backstop, run against the buffer alone. History is aged out by a separate retention clock and nothing else. The two never intersect.

So when the attempts-drop abandons a recipient, the outbox rows it was owed get reclaimed, but the messages themselves sit untouched in history. Recovery is client-pull, not server-push: on reconnect the client asks for everything since the last id it saw —

GET /api/chats/{id}/messages?since=<last-seen-id>

— and reads it straight from history. The server never has to hold the delivery open, and the buffer is free to drain. This makes the abandon cheap, which is the whole reason we can afford to be aggressive with it. The one contract it imposes: a client must resync on reconnect. Since the attempts-drop deletes the very rows a naive poller would have re-pushed, a client that relies only on live delivery would miss them; the since-cursor sync is what closes the loop.

The proof that this is working, at load, is a single number: deliveries per accepted message ≈ 1.0. When that ratio climbs toward 2, the system is re-delivering — the waterline is stuck and the poller is churning. Held at 1.0, every message is delivered essentially once, the buffer stays bounded, and reconnects are the honest cost of the clients that genuinely fell behind.

06 — The control surface

Every queue, and what it is for

Throughput at this shape is not one dial; it is a set of bounded buffers, each protecting a different stage from the one before it. The design keeps them explicit and separately tunable rather than hidden behind a single thread pool.

The persist and delivery pools are two separate reactive connection pools over the same Postgres, isolated so a burst of writes can never starve live reads. Each batcher exists to turn many small, latency-bound operations into few throughput-bound ones — the recurring move whenever a database pool would otherwise sit idle waiting on round trips.

07 — The numbers

What the load campaign settled

The configuration above is not folklore; it is what survived a sweep. Six hundred clients messaging each other pairwise at a sustained two thousand messages a second — well past any real peak — with every knob moved in isolation and the run reconciled message-by-message. The verdict was unusually crisp.

Read top to bottom, it is the same lesson each time: anything that delays the drop — grace above zero, a lease longer than the GC cadence, a pending cap so low it reaps the healthy — trades a bounded buffer and a 1.0 delivery ratio for a re-delivery storm. The aggressive, immediate drop is not a compromise; it is the optimum.

08 — The horizon

A rehearsal for the log

Every decision here was made with one eye on the thing we haven't built yet. The outbox is a partitioned log drawn in a relational table; the BIGSERIAL is an offset; the Tetris watermark is a consumer's committed position. When the messenger moves onto Kafka, the mapping is nearly mechanical:

  • The shared outbox becomes a topic partitioned by recipient — so each key's progress is independent and there is no single cross-key minimum to freeze.
  • The global watermark becomes a per-partition committed offset. Reclaim stops being a delete gated on the slowest consumer and becomes time-and-size retention, decoupled from consumption entirely — a laggard can no longer hold anyone's floor down.
  • message_history does not move. It is already the durable truth; the log only ever replaced the buffer.

At that point the head-of-line block dissolves for free, the attempts-drop becomes a seek() instead of a broken socket, and the reconnect churn we pay today disappears — because a slow consumer resumes from its offset rather than being dropped and told to resync. The single global minimum, the one weakness, is the exact thing the partitioned log is designed to remove.

Until then, we keep the buffer honest: one table, one waterline, an aggressive drop, and a durable history to fall back on.
